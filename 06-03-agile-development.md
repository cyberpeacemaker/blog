## The Old Way vs. The Agile Way

Before Agile, the industry used something called the **Waterfall** method. It mirrored physical construction: you draw the blueprints, lay the foundation, build the walls, and put on the roof. You cannot build the roof first. In software, this meant spending months writing 500-page requirements documents before writing a single line of code.

Here is how the two approaches compare:

| Feature | The Old Way ("Waterfall") | The Agile Way |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Planning** | Plan 100% upfront before coding. | Plan the basics, start coding, adjust as you go. |
| **Flexibility** | Changes ruin the timeline and budget. | Changes are expected, welcomed, and planned for. |
| **Delivery** | Users see the final product after a year. | Users test a basic working version in a few weeks. |
| **Risk** | You might spend a year building something nobody wants. | You fail fast, pivot quickly, and waste less time. |

## How Agile Works in Practice

Agile is not a specific software tool; it is a mindset and a set of habits. When developers "do Agile," they usually follow a few core concepts:

* **Sprints:** Instead of having one massive deadline eight months from now, you break the work into small cycles called "Sprints" (usually 1 to 2 weeks long). You pick a small chunk of features, build them, test them, and finish them within that week.
* **The Minimum Viable Product (MVP):** If your goal is to build a car, the old way would build a wheel, then an axle, then an engine. You can't drive it until it's 100% finished. The Agile way builds a skateboard first. It's basic, but it gets you from A to B. Next sprint, you upgrade it to a scooter, then a bicycle, then a car. You always have a working product.
* **Constant Feedback Loops:** Because you are building things in small 2-week chunks, you can constantly show your work to your users, clients, or team. If they say, "Actually, we hate this feature," you only wasted two weeks of work, not a whole year.
* **Refactoring as a Rule:** In Agile, reorganizing your code (like you are doing now) isn't a sign that you "failed to plan." It is a healthy, expected part of the process. We call it "refactoring." You clean the house as you live in it.

Ultimately, Agile admits a fundamental truth: **You know the least about a project on the very first day.** It makes no sense to make all your permanent decisions when you have the least amount of information!
