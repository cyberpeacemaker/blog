It’s a variation of the classic "less is more" rule, but it cuts much deeper. What it's really saying is: **If your design relies on a chaotic rainbow to get its point across, you lack a cohesive visual identity.**

When you dilute a project with four, five, or six different colors, they all start competing for the viewer's attention. Instead of creating variety, you create noise. The colors cancel each other out, resulting in a muddy, forgettable visual experience—hence, you effectively have *"zero colors."*

### The Magic of Three: The 60-30-10 Rule

In design, interior decorating, and branding, the three-color limit usually breaks down into a perfect harmony:

* **60% Dominant Color (The Canvas):** Usually a neutral background that sets the overall mood.
* **30% Secondary Color (The Structure):** Creates contrast and defines the primary elements or typography.
* **10% Accent Color (The Punch):** Used sparingly to draw the eye *exactly* where you want it (like a "Buy Now" button or a critical warning).

If you add a fourth or fifth color into that mix, the 10% accent loses its power, and the viewer no longer knows where to look.

### Exceptions to the Rule?

Of course, rules are made to be broken—but only if you know *how* to break them.

* **Data Visualization:** Complex charts and maps often require more than three colors to differentiate data streams.
* **Maximalism/Illustration:** If the *goal* is to evoke overwhelming energy or nostalgia (think Google, eBay, or 90s aesthetic), a broader palette works.

But for branding, UI/UX, and daily design work, keeping it to a strict trio forces you to rely on hierarchy, contrast, and layout rather than using color as a crutch.
