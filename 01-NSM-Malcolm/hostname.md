In the context of **Malcolm** and **OpenSearch**, `host.name` refers to the name of the **network sensor or machine that observed and collected the data**.

Because Malcolm relies on OpenSearch as its backend data store and uses the **Elastic Common Schema (ECS)** to standardize its data, `host.name` serves a specific and vital role in tracking where your network telemetry is coming from.

Here is a breakdown of exactly what it means and how it is used:

### 1. The Malcolm-Specific Meaning (The Network Sensor)

In a Malcolm deployment—especially distributed ones—you often have multiple network sensors (running packet capture tools like Zeek or Arkime) forwarding traffic logs back to a central Malcolm instance.

* **Sensor Identification:** `host.name` tells you *which specific sensor* captured that piece of network traffic.
* **Health and Ingestion:** Malcolm uses this field to track ingestion statistics. By querying `host.name`, you can easily see the last time a specific sensor successfully forwarded logs to the database.

### 2. The OpenSearch / ECS Definition

Under the hood, OpenSearch expects data formatted according to the Elastic Common Schema. In ECS, `host.name` is a core field defined as:

* **The Host Name:** It typically contains exactly what the `hostname` command returns on a Unix/Linux system.
* **Format:** It is usually the lowercase fully qualified domain name (FQDN) of the host (e.g., `sensor-dmz-01.local` or simply `sensor-dmz-01`).

### How to use it in Malcolm

When you are building dashboards or filtering traffic in OpenSearch Dashboards (the UI Malcolm uses), you can use `host.name` to:

* **Filter Traffic:** E.g., `host.name: "factory-floor-sensor"` will filter your view to only show network traffic captured by the sensor on the factory floor.
* **Troubleshoot:** If you notice a sudden drop in logs, grouping your data by `host.name` will immediately show you which capture node went offline.

---

**In short:** If you are looking at a log entry in Malcolm, `host.name` is the name of the appliance/sensor that was "watching" the network when that event happened.

---

It is completely normal to be confused by this! The terminology in network security monitoring overlaps a lot, and it is very easy to mix up the hardware, the software, and the logs themselves.

Here is the easiest way to untangle it: **Think of the difference between "The Box" and "The Software."**

### 1. The Box (`host.name`)

The `host.name` is the actual, literal computer (or virtual machine) that is plugged into your network.

* In your case, the server or virtual machine running the packet capture was given the operating system name **"malcolm"**.
* If you had three different capture computers placed in different buildings, their `host.name`s might be `sensor-nyc`, `sensor-london`, and `sensor-tokyo`.

### 2. The Software (Zeek, Suricata, Arkime)

Zeek, Suricata, and Arkime are **software programs** running *inside* that box. They are not the "host" itself; they are the engines doing the heavy lifting.

* **Zeek** is the program that watches the traffic and writes down metadata (like a receptionist keeping a visitor log).
* **Suricata** is the program that watches the traffic and compares it against a list of known bad signatures (like a security guard looking for known criminals).

### 3. The Logs

The logs are the actual text files and data records generated by those software programs.

**An Analogy:**
Think of a restaurant kitchen.

* **`host.name` (malcolm)** is the Kitchen itself.
* **Zeek and Suricata** are the Chefs working in the kitchen.
* **The Logs** are the meals they cook and hand to you.

When the dashboard says the "Log Source" is `malcolm`, it is simply saying, *"This log was cooked in the Malcolm kitchen."*

### Why the dashboard looks the way it does

Take a look at the bottom left of your screenshot again. You will see **"zeek - Logs"** and **"suricata - Logs"**. The dashboard *does* differentiate between them! It just classifies them as the *type* of log, rather than the `host.name`.
