In Latin, the phrase *ad hoc* literally translates to "for this."

In the workplace, an **ad-hoc request** is an unplanned, one-off task that pops up outside of your normal routine, scheduled workflow, or official job duties. They are the sudden "can you just do this quickly" favors that inevitably interrupt your day.

## Key Characteristics

Ad-hoc requests usually share a few common traits:

* **Unplanned:** They aren't on your calendar, project board, or weekly to-do list. They arrive randomly via a desk drop-by, a Slack/Teams message, or a frantic email.
* **One-time only:** They are typically needed for a specific, isolated situation, not a recurring process you can automate or plan for.
* **Artificially urgent:** Because the person asking usually needs it immediately for a meeting or a deadline, these requests often come with a high sense of urgency (even if it's not a true emergency).

## Common Examples

* **Data/Reporting:** "Hey, I have a client call in ten minutes. Can you pull a custom list of all users who bought X product last month?"
* **Review/Feedback:** "Could you just put eyes on this slide deck real quick before I send it to the CEO?"
* **Creative/Design:** "Can we quickly swap the blue background to green on this graphic? It should only take a second."
* **IT/Support:** "My printer isn't working, can you come look at it?" (When there is an official IT ticket system they ignored).

## The Problem with Ad-Hoc Requests

While being flexible and helping coworkers is a good thing, a high volume of ad-hoc requests is a massive productivity killer.

Every time you stop your planned work to answer a "quick question" or do a "five-minute favor," you suffer from **context switching**. It can take your brain up to 20 minutes to get back into the flow of your original, deep work. If a team's day is entirely driven by ad-hoc requests, planned projects inevitably fall behind schedule.
